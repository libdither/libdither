How the model works, and what it leaves out

What's being modelled

A textbook monocentric city (Alonso 1964; Muth 1969; Mills 1967), the model Bertaud & Brueckner (2005) used to estimate what Bangalore's floor-area (FAR) limits cost residents: 3–6% of household consumption, with a footprint about 17% smaller in their simulation without the limits. Every household works downtown. Rent per sq ft falls with distance by exactly enough to offset the longer commute, so nobody wants to move. On each lot, builders choose how much floor space to build: taller is worth it only where rents cover the rising cost of building tall. Land stays farmland wherever farming, plus the saved cost of new roads and pipes, beats urban land rent, and that sets the edge of the city.

How it maps onto the play-dough

  • The dough is floor space. In the 3D view, each block's volume is the floor space on that land.
  • The brown paper is the land the free-market city occupies. Green is farmland.
  • The lid caps building height, drawn in stories on half the lot. The hole exempts downtown. Parking minimums and growth boundaries are the other rules.
  • Play-dough can't be compressed and can't leave the table. Those are two separate assumptions, and each gets a slider. With both at zero the model conserves floor space exactly, like the dough. Loosening either lets squeezed floor space go unbuilt: people live in less space, or live somewhere else.

Calibration

Household preferences are Stone–Geary, calibrated so a household spends 30% of an $85k income on ≈1,600 sq ft at typical rents, with the chosen price elasticity (which is also the income elasticity). By default the cost of height matches the per-floor cost increases Ahlfeldt & McMillen (2018, Table 6) report at 2.5, ≈15 and 110 floors: ≈10%, ≈4% and ≈1.6% per floor. Their figures are sample-average slopes and the supertall one rests on engineering estimates, so the “cost of building tall” slider can flatten the curve. Low-density housing is Cobb–Douglas in land and capital with a 20% land share. A surface parking space takes 30 m² (≈320 sq ft) including aisles; a garage space costs ≈$30k. Streets, parks and non-residential uses take 40% of urban land.

What it leaves out

  • Jobs outside downtown, traffic congestion, and the shops and offices that dominate real downtown skylines.
  • Different incomes, so no “luxury condo” effect. Downtown gets taller only because space is scarce.
  • Time. Real buildings last decades, so a city adjusts to new rules slowly. This model shows the long-run end state.
  • Limits on homes per lot and minimum lot sizes, which is how single-family zoning actually works. The lid here caps floor space instead; with flexible home sizes the two aren't equivalent.
  • Why people like yards and cars. The free-market city here is roughly 3–4× denser than real US metros of the same size, and the rules explain only part of that gap. Cheap car commuting is a big part of the rest (Glaeser & Kahn 2004). Try the commuting-cost slider.
  • Parking people would want anyway. The parking slider treats every required space as unwanted, so read it as an upper bound.
